Decision

Use the dashboard to prioritize source-record and identity review. Do not interpret award commitments as payments, rankings as verified supplier performance, or hypothetical sensitivity scenarios as savings.

Case 1 — A source instruction had become a supplier

The upgraded rules identify 193 placeholder links and 1,093 malformed registration links across the normalized source. Examples include attachment-list instructions and generic labels such as “Various.” They remain in headline award totals but cannot carry supplier shares.

Using the same pinned archives, combined identity/evidence improvements changed attributable value from £325.90bn (32.9% of eligible value) to £236.35bn (23.9%). The difference is a change in analytical attribution, not reduced procurement spend or measured savings. Exact reviewed approvals also restore selected legitimate names, so the difference must not be attributed to one exclusion rule alone.

The versioned evidence review contains 31 targeted registration/name cases: 11 approvals, 2 rejections and 18 unresolved cases. Five Companies House records anchor the accepted exact names. This is not a random test set or an independent accuracy audit.

Random validation — a frozen registry sample

A deterministic sample of 40 from 11,517 previously unreviewed GB-COH identities produced 36 compatible registry-name records, 2 name conflicts and 2 unobserved records. Confirmed agreement is 90.0%, with a 95% Wilson interval of 76.9%–96.0%. Missing-evidence bounds are 90.0%–95.0%. The sample was frozen before its registry observations; subsequent conflict holdbacks are documented remediation, not a claim of independently measured post-remediation accuracy.

This is registry-name consistency in a restricted registration-evidence frame, not precision/recall of all supplier matching. Name-only identities, source assignment errors despite a matching legal name, and independent professional validation remain outside this result.

Case 3 — Related awards change the stability assessment

For the leading supported buyer identity by attributable value, Home Office, 311 awards belong to 266 procurement processes. R independently reproduces HHI 1,113. The process-clustered percentile interval is 622–3,313; the independent-award alternative is 626–2,521. These intervals are descriptive, with 300 draws under each sampling assumption.

Removing the largest award gives HHI 790; buyer-specific 99th-percentile capping gives 541. Registration evidence covers 0.0% of this buyer identity's attributable value. Neither the ranking nor interval is a guarantee of operational supplier dependency. Review identity confidence and individual commitments before making a procurement decision.

Decision worksheet — should this buyer enter a supplier-risk review?

Question: does Home Office's observed portfolio justify immediately changing supplier strategy? Recommendation: prioritize verification, not a supplier change. The process interval spans 622–3,313; the largest-award sensitivity changes the point estimate from 1,113 to 790. That instability and 0.0% registration-evidence coverage make an operational dependency claim premature.

Analyst handoff: download buyer metrics and buyer sensitivity. Match buyer_key b49f4c311ff916ace705f91d to the case's 20-largest-attributable-commitments download; buyer names alone are not unique. This is a selected evidence queue, not the complete 311-award portfolio. Inspect its original notice links and identity basis. Investigation brief — evidence, options and handoff

The workspace links this case to publication ID b49f4c311ff916ace705f91d, not every record named Home Office. Its evidence-review priority is 35/100, assigned by explicit coverage, registration, commitment-size and concentration-sensitivity rules. This is not a supplier-risk score, the queue's highest-priority case, or a validated prediction.

Options considered: immediately change the supplier strategy; dismiss the finding because the buyer passes the coverage gate; or investigate the evidence gaps first. The supported choice is the third. Coverage measures eligible commitments represented in attribution; it does not verify legal identity or actual service dependency. The R interval and largest-award sensitivity show why a single point estimate is not sufficient evidence for intervention.

Proposed handoff: an analyst opens the exact buyer profile, inspects supplier shares and original notices, and records unresolved identity/payment/framework questions as awaiting evidence. A procurement owner supplies legal identifiers, payments and service-criticality context. A reviewer records the conclusion only after inspecting the evidence; closure requires an evidence URL and no unresolved questions in the form. Reopening appends another entry and preserves the prior decision. No review entry, stakeholder endorsement or implemented supplier change is prepopulated or claimed.

Completion criteria: reconcile the intended legal buyer and supplier identities; resolve material overlaps between framework ceilings and call-offs; define the actual payment period; and obtain a procurement owner's assessment of critical services and substitute availability. If evidence remains unavailable, retain the case awaiting evidence rather than reporting savings or confirmed operational risk. The demonstrated outcome is a reproducible investigation and handoff, not measured organizational impact.

The latter yield 134 current/former-name agreements, 21 cases requiring review and 5 unavailable pages. These are not additional human-reviewed accuracy labels. A versioned follow-up queue preserves nonmatches and unavailable evidence; no attribution rule changes automatically.
